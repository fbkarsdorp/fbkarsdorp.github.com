Comparative analyses of diversity in human populations often rely on historical, archival and observational data, which are systematically shaped by
uneven documentation, preservation and research attention. Such collection
bias can distort comparisons across populations, regions or time periods,
obscuring genuine patterns of variation or generating spurious ones. Standard approaches that equalize sample size or sampling effort implicitly assume comparable sampling completeness, which is an assumption rarely
satisfied in human and historical datasets. Here, we evaluate coveragebased standardization, adapted from ecological diversity estimation, as a
general framework for comparative diversity analysis under biased and incomplete observation. Using population-level simulations, we show that
coverage-based approaches reliably recover true diversity relationships
across multiple, qualitatively distinct mechanisms of collection bias,
whereas sample-size-based methods yield systematically distorted inferences. We illustrate the utility of this framework with an application to a
large historical cultural dataset, where correcting for uneven documentation substantially refines inferred diversity patterns. By conditioning comparisons on sampling completeness rather than raw sample size, coveragebased standardization offers a principled and broadly applicable solution
for comparative research in any domain where observation is biased, incomplete or historically contingent.

Comparative analyses of diversity in human populations rely heavily on historical, archival and observational data, ranging from archaeological assemblages
and historical records to linguistic corpora and cultural databases. Such data
are almost invariably shaped by uneven documentation, preservation and research attention, producing systematic collection biases that vary across populations, time periods and social contexts. As a result, apparent differences in
diversity may reflect how evidence was collected rather than how diversity
was distributed in the underlying populations. This challenge is not unique
to human data. Similar forms of biased observation arise across ecology, evolutionary biology and the study of past biodiversity, where sampling effort,

Collection bias arises from a range of interacting mechanisms—both material and human—that shape what gets documented
and preserved in empirical records [3]. Material factors play a key role: in natural history collections, specimen size and preservation quality directly influence their likelihood of collection, while in archaeological and cultural datasets, artefact durability and
visibility shape documentation patterns [4,5]. At the same time, human choices, either driven by expertise, institutional priorities
or aesthetic preferences, introduce additional layers of systematic distortion [6]. In folklore research, for instance, collectors often
privileged certain genres (e.g. supernatural narratives) or guided documentation toward specific forms, shaping the resulting
record according to their research aims [7,8]. Such decisions produce enduring imbalances that reflect the priorities of collectors
more than the structure of the underlying original systems.
Spatial and temporal factors further compound these biases by introducing systematic variation in documentation intensity
across regions and historical periods [9]. Proximity to research centres, accessibility of sites and fluctuations in institutional resources all shape where, when and how data are collected. These patterns do not operate in isolation: their interaction can produce
complex distortions, such as the underrepresentation of remote populations or temporal clustering that exaggerates or obscures
continuity [6,10]. As a result, even well-intended documentation efforts may yield datasets that systematically misrepresent the
structure of the underlying system they aim to document.
These biasing mechanisms pose particular challenges for comparative diversity research, where researchers seek to relate observed
repertoire size to explanatory variables, such as population size, productivity or spatial connectivity. Because documentation
intensity often correlates with the very factors under investigation, collection bias can systematically distort comparative inference.
For example, studies examining how population size shapes cultural or biological diversity [11] may overestimate demographic
effects if larger populations receive disproportionate documentation attention. More generally, biased observation can generate
apparent diversity gradients that reflect collection practices rather than underlying generative processes [1].
Two particularly pervasive patterns of collection bias stem from the implicit risk-reward trade-offs that shape documentation
strategies across domains. Collectors, often operating under limited resources, allocate effort towards locations, phenomena or
communities perceived as especially promising or distinctive. These behavioural dynamics have been documented in archaeology,
palaeontology, folklore and musicology, and can systematically distort observed diversity patterns. Treating such dynamics as
recurrent, structured sources of bias allows their effects to be modelled and evaluated within comparative analyses.
The first of these patterns, often termed the ‘productivity paradox’, refers to collectors’ tendency to concentrate effort in already
productive locations [3,6]. This self-reinforcing process leads to dense documentation of a subset of sites or populations, not
necessarily because they are more diverse, but because they are more accessible or already well known. In folklore research, for
example, reliance on initial informant networks often produced dense local documentation around early contact points [7]. In
comparative analyses, such clustering can generate artificial diversity patterns driven by effort rather than underlying variation.
A second pattern, known as Stromer’s Riddle [12], involves the systematic overrepresentation of rare or distinctive items. In
palaeontology, for instance, theropod dinosaurs appear disproportionately frequently in collections relative to their inferred ecological abundance [3,12]. Analogous processes operate in cultural datasets. Medieval manuscripts illustrate this clearly: while
ordinary manuscripts have survival rates of roughly 8%, luxuriously decorated manuscripts survive at rates approaching 20%
[13,14]. Because such items were originally rarer, their disproportionate preservation distorts reconstructions of historical
diversity.
Together, these patterns illustrate how collection bias can distort diversity estimates along precisely the dimensions scholars
aim to compare. To address this problem, we introduce coverage-based standardization as a general framework for comparative
diversity inference under biased and incomplete observation. Adapted from ecological diversity estimation, this approach enables
comparisons across assemblages with differing documentation intensity by conditioning inference on sampling completeness
rather than sample size. We first use simulations to examine how distinct mechanisms of collection bias, such as preferential
sampling of productive sites or overrepresentation of rare variants, distort diversity estimates under conventional methods. We
then apply coverage-based standardization to a historical cultural dataset on seventeenth-century Dutch song culture, examining
how population size relates to melodic diversity across urban centres. Together, these analyses demonstrate that coverage-based
standardization can recover diversity patterns that would otherwise be obscured or distorted by collection bias, providing a robust
foundation for comparative research across domains.

To simulate populations where transmission between individuals occurs in a neutral, unbiased way, we use the Wright–Fisher
model. In this model, a population of fixed size N evolves in discrete, non-overlapping generations: each new generation is formed
by sampling N variants with replacement from the previous generation, so variant counts follow a multinomial distribution. With
small probability 𝜇 each sampling event introduces a novel variant not previously seen in the population.
In empirical studies, however, complete population data are rarely available; researchers typically observe only a sample drawn
from one or several populations. Naturally, the sampling process can be subject to a variety of biases and here we ask how different documentation practices (in the following, sampling strategies) affect our ability to infer relative population diversity from
samples.
We simulate a series of eight neutrally evolving populations with the same innovation rate 𝜇 and exponentially increasing
sizes following a geometric progression from 1000 to 128,000 individuals by factors of two (i.e. N ∈ {1000, 2000, … , 128, 000}). So
populations differ in size but have evolved neutrally with the same innovation rate 𝜇. To generate samples, we treat sampling
as a sequential two-stage process, which is repeated until the desired overall sample size is reached. At each step, we (i) select a
population according to the sampling strategy under consideration, and (ii) select a variant from that population according to the
within-population selection rule specified by the strategy.
This process yields samples of varying size from different populations, depending on both the total sampling budget and the
sampling strategy used. Our approach therefore simulates both the underlying evolutionary process affecting the composition of
the populations and the collection process, following the ‘virtual ecologist’ framework [21], which allows systematic evaluation
of how sampling biases affect the measurement of diversity patterns.

more complex feedback mechanisms that characterize actual collection processes, such as the above-described ‘productivity paradox’ (preferential sampling of already-documented locations) and ‘Stromer’s Riddle’ (over-representation of rare variants).

4. Population size and diversity: theory and a historical case study
The relationship between population size and diversity is a central question in ecology, evolutionary biology and cultural evolution, and a common target of comparative inference. In cultural evolution, the relationship between population size and cultural
complexity is one of the most extensively studied topics [11]. A rich theoretical framework predicts how demographic conditions shape the emergence, transmission and maintenance of cultural traits across populations [22,23]. At its core, this framework
posits two complementary mechanisms. First, larger populations provide more opportunities for innovation, not only through
sheer numbers but also by enabling more frequent recombination and exchange. Second, larger populations create more stable
conditions for maintaining innovations once they appear, reducing random loss through drift, providing more opportunities for
transmission and potentially supporting greater specialization [23–26].

Asia [22,24]. Comparative studies of contemporary populations have further strengthened this empirical foundation, revealing
positive correlations between population size and the diversity and complexity of traditional toolkits [29,30]. Historical cases, such

Recent research shows that demographic effects can vary substantially across cultural domains [35]. In linguistic evolution, for
instance, larger populations are associated with more lexical diversity but simpler grammatical structures [36,37]. In expressive
culture, the relationship appears even more complex. Studies of folktales suggest that larger populations exhibit greater diversity in
tale types but less complexity in narrative structure [38]. Musical traditions may follow similar patterns: larger populations might
promote diversity, while aesthetic norms drive convergence toward certain levels of complexity [39]. These findings suggest that
expressive domains may follow different evolutionary trajectories than technological ones that are shaped not only by transmission
fidelity and innovation rate, but by preferences, audience effects and communicative constraints.
In general, translating these theoretical predictions into empirical tests faces two distinct challenges. First, the theoretically
relevant demographic quantity is not census population size but the culturally effective population size, which depends on transmission biases, network structure and the concentration of cultural influence [40]. Second, in historical and observational datasets,
larger populations often leave more extensive records, not necessarily because they ere ::
are:more culturally diverse, but because
they attracted more documentation effort or left behind more durable artefacts. As a result, attempts to study population-size
effects through historical sources are especially vulnerable to confounding, and distinguishing genuine demographic effects from
sampling artefacts remains a major methodological challenge.
In this section, we first show theoretically that collection biases in form of population-size-biased sampling can strongly distort estimated scaling between population size and cultural diversity, so empirical studies must account for this bias. We then

1600, melodic diversity appears higher than the population size alone would predict. Historical demographic research by [47]
provides context for this period: Amsterdam was on the cusp of a major demographic transformation, with marriages doubling
between 1601 and 1603. This surge marked the beginning of a new cultural market, particularly among wealthy young couples.
This demographic shift created a substantial cohort of relatively affluent young adults who could engage with musical culture. The
initially high diversity in 1600, followed by continued growth through the 1610s and 1620s as population increased, suggests that
Amsterdam’s cultural vibrancy preceded and then kept pace with its demographic expansion. This pattern might reflect the early

through new forms of songbooks starting in 1602.
Taken together, our cross-sectional and diachronic results show that collection biases can substantially influence the apparent
scaling between cultural diversity and population size in raw abundance data. Therefore, coverage-based standardization should
be considered as a routine step to obtain more reliable inferences.

datasets may reflect patterns of collection rather than properties of the underlying systems they aim to describe. These distortions
are especially problematic when they align with the dimensions researchers seek to compare, such as population size, region,
productivity or historical period, risking spurious inferences or masking genuine effects. Addressing these challenges therefore
requires inferential frameworks that explicitly account for biased and incomplete observation, and help distinguish signal from
artefact in the (historical) record.
Coverage-based standardization provides a principled approach for comparing diversity across assemblages that differ in documentation intensity. Originally developed in ecological research [18,19,48], this framework shifts the focus from equalizing sample size to equalizing sampling completeness, defined as the proportion of the underlying repertoire represented in the sample.
When two assemblages are compared at the same coverage level, they are understood—by construction—to represent equivalent
fractions of their respective underlying diversity, even if different absolute sample sizes were required to achieve that completeness. Our simulation results demonstrate that, whereas conventional diversity estimates are highly sensitive to collection effort,
coverage-based standardization reliably recovers true diversity relationships across a wide range of biased sampling regimes.
This robustness makes it particularly valuable for historical, archaeological and archival data, where observation is shaped by
processes largely outside the analyst’s control.
A key contribution of our analysis is showing that this robustness extends beyond simple differences in sampling effort to qualitatively different forms of collection bias. Our simulations incorporate three empirically grounded mechanisms: (i) populationsize-biased sampling, (ii) productivity-based reinforcement (‘productivity paradox’), and rarity-biased documentation (‘Stromer’s
Riddle’). Each of these biases distorts samples in distinct ways: by (i) redistributing effort towards large or small populations, by
(ii) amplifying early chance discoveries (‘rich-get-richer’ dynamics), or (iii) by disproportionately targeting rare variants. Our results show that on average, coverage-based standardization reliably recovered the true relative richness profiles underlying these
biased samples. By contrast, uncorrected diversity estimates were systematically skewed, and rarefaction-based standardization
performed even worse. These results show that coverage-based standardization does not merely correct for unequal sample sizes,
but can neutralize a broad class of biases that jointly affect where observation occurs and which variants are recorded. This capacity
is crucial for comparative analyses based on heterogeneous and historically contingent data.
We illustrated the practical implications of this framework using a historical dataset on seventeenth-century Dutch song culture.
Under conventional analysis, we observed a strong positive association between population size and melodic diversity, consistent
with theoretical expectations but potentially inflated by uneven documentation. After applying coverage-based standardization,
this relationship remained positive but was substantially attenuated. Importantly, coverage standardization preserves the functional form of demographic scaling: because standardization rescales diversity by a constant factor determined by the chosen coverage level, the scaling exponent relating diversity to population size remains invariant in expectation. The corrected estimates
therefore retain the demographic signal while reducing inflation caused by collection bias. In this case, the results support the
view that larger populations tend to support greater musical diversity, while also showing that traditional methods can overstate
the strength of this relationship.
Our case study also sheds light on why prior research into expressive cultural domains, such as music, folklore and literature, has often produced inconsistent findings. Many earlier studies relied on presence-absence data, which cannot account for
differences in documentation intensity across populations. As our results show, this limitation can inflate apparent effects in welldocumented contexts or obscure them in underrepresented ones. By incorporating abundance data and applying coverage-based
standardization, we clarify the demographic signal while controlling for bias. Although collecting such data requires more effort
than incidence-based approaches, even relatively modest samples can yield reliable diversity estimates: as Robbins [49] shows,
the mean squared error of the coverage estimator decreases rapidly with sample size. Our simulations reinforce this point (cf. §2
of the supplementary material): for sample sizes above roughly 30 observations, coverage estimates and diversity ratios converge
tightly around their expected values. This means that researchers can obtain stable, meaningful diversity estimates even from
limited archival samples, so long as they retain frequency information.
The relevance of coverage-based standardization extends well beyond demographic scaling. Many central questions in comparative research involve contrasts across assemblages that differ systematically in observation intensity, including comparisons
across historical periods, regions, genres, media or social strata. Temporal analyses must contend with unequal preservation and
survival—for example, differences between manuscript and print cultures [50] or challenges posed by digital ephemerality [51,52].
Genre-based comparisons face analogous issues, as written traditions are often better documented than oral ones [53,54], and elite
cultural production is more likely to be preserved than vernacular or everyday forms [55]. In all such cases, coverage-based standardization offers a principled way to condition comparisons on sampling completeness, shifting the question from how much was
recorded to how much of what existed was recorded, and thereby bringing comparative inference closer to the phenomena it aims to
describe.
