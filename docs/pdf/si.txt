Rarefaction-based diversity

Rarefaction-based standardization equalizes sampling effort by reducing all collections to the size of the
smallest. While widely used, this approach introduces systematic distortions when collection intensity
varies across populations. As Figure 1 shows, rarefaction systematically compresses relative richness
ratios, particularly among larger populations, especially when sample sizes are small. Crucially, in
all conditions rarefaction-based standardization performs worse than taking the uncorrected diversity
estimates based on the observed sample sizes.

To understand basic properties of coverage-based standardization, we use the Wright-Fisher model. In
this model, a population of fixed size N evolves in discrete, non-overlapping generations: each new
generation is formed by sampling N cultural variants with replacement from the previous generation,
so variant counts follow a multinomial distribution. With a small probability µ each sampling event
introduces a novel variant not previously seen in the population.

2.2

Accuracy of diversity ratio estimation under coverage-based standardization

The idea of coverage-based standardization is that samples with similar coverage should yield a richness
ratio which should approximate the true ratio of the underlying populations. More precisely, when
comparing two samples coverage-based standardization proceeds by determining their shared coverage
level Ĉmax = min{Ĉ1 , Ĉ2 }, where Ĉi is the coverage estimate (3) for sample i and calculating the ratio
of the corresponding richness values S1 (Ĉmax ) and S2 (Ĉmax ).
In the last section we showed that for small sample sizes the realized coverage level can vary substantially around its mean. We now examine how that variability propagates to the shared coverage
level Ĉmax between two sample and how variability in Ĉmax translates into uncertainty in the richness
ratio estimate S1 (Ĉmax ) and S2 (Ĉmax ). We aim to provide a rough guideline on sample sizes required
for coverage-based standardization to be reliable.

Step 2: Determine Ĉmax . To compare the two cities at equal sampling completeness, we set Ĉmax

Step 3: Subsample to Ĉmax . Bruges’ sample already has coverage 0.52, so it remains unchanged,

SLeiden (Ĉmax ) = 256 melodies.
When comparing more than two assemblages (as in Figure 6 of the main text), the same procedure
applies. We compute Ĉn for each city and set Ĉmax to the minimum coverage across all assemblages. In
the cross-sectional analysis, Ĉmax = 0.52, determined by Bruges (Table 3). Every other city’s sample is
then rarefied to this common coverage level, and the resulting richness values are those plotted on the
y-axis of the right panel of Figure 6.
